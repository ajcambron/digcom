# 2.3 | Transitions & Editing Conventions

## Key Terms

{: .vocab }
{% include vocab/add-transitions-conventions.md %}

A rough cut assembled from clips isn't the same as a sequence that *feels* smooth. Today's
techniques are what separate the two — small, deliberate choices about exactly where and how a
cut happens.

## Direct Instruction (~10 min)

{% include worksheet.html file="add2-transitions-conventions-reference" title="2.3 Guided Notes: Transitions & Conventions" presentation="/assets/presentations/add2-3.html" %}

> "An L cut lets a voice keep talking while we've already cut to what they're talking about — it's
> one of the most common techniques in every interview-style video you've ever watched, and once
> you know to look for it, you'll see it everywhere."

Walk through: **continuity editing** (keeping the viewer oriented, not noticing the cuts), the
**180-degree rule**, **L cuts** and **J cuts**, **match cuts**, **cutting on the action**,
**cutaways**, **montage**, and the **jump cut** (usually a mistake, not a choice).

**Scaffolded questions — cold-call through all three:**

> **Recall:** "What's the difference between an L cut and a J cut?"

> **Analysis:** "Why does a cutaway help an editor hide an awkward edit point?"

> **Synthesis/Extension:** "Find a jump cut in something you've watched recently — was it a
> mistake, or a deliberate style choice? How can you tell?"

## Guided Practice — Revise Your Rough Cut (~23 min)

1. **Open your rough cut** from 2.2.
2. **Add an L or J cut** somewhere audio and video don't need to change at exactly the same frame
   — let one lead or trail the other.
3. **Insert a cutaway** to cover a rough edit point or add context.
4. **Check for accidental jump cuts** — if two adjacent shots of the same subject look nearly
   identical, fix or intentionally justify it.
5. **Turn and Talk:** swap seats with a partner and identify which technique they used where,
   without being told first.

{: .highlight }
An L/J cut only "works" if the audio and video are genuinely offset from each other on the
timeline — if they still start and stop at the same frame, you haven't actually made one yet.

{: .discussion }
**Cold call check:** you're editing a two-person interview and the same speaker's face is on
screen for both their question and their answer. What continuity technique would make that feel
less static?
