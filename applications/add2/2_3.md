# 2.3 | Editing Basics & Continuity Conventions

## Key Terms

{: .vocab }
{% include vocab/add-editing-basics.md %}

{: .vocab }
{% include vocab/add-transitions-conventions.md %}

Non-linear editing means every edit you make is really just an instruction. The original
footage never changes, so you can rearrange, trim, and undo endlessly without losing quality.
Today you'll learn the core moves editors make hundreds of times an hour, then use them to build
a sequence that actually feels smooth, not just assembled.

## Direct Instruction (~15 min)

{% include worksheet.html file="add2-editing-basics-reference" title="2.3 Guided Notes: Editing Basics" presentation="/assets/presentations/add2-3.html" %}

> "J, K, L. Backward, pause, forward. Tap L twice for double speed. This is how every professional
> editor moves through footage, because reaching for a mouse every time is too slow."

Walk through: the **timeline**, the **playhead**, **JKL navigation**, setting **In and Out points**
with `I` and `O`, and the four ways to get a clip onto the timeline. **Insert** (pushes everything
later), **Overwrite** (records over what's there), **Lift** (removes, leaves a gap), **Extract**
(removes, closes the gap).

{% include worksheet.html file="add2-transitions-conventions-reference" title="2.3 Guided Notes: Transitions & Conventions" %}

> "An L cut lets a voice keep talking while we've already cut to what they're talking about. It's
> one of the most common techniques in every interview-style video you've ever watched, and once
> you know to look for it, you'll see it everywhere."

Then: **continuity editing** (keeping the viewer oriented, not noticing the cuts), the
**180-degree rule**, **L cuts** and **J cuts**, **match cuts**, **cutting on the action**,
**cutaways**, **montage**, and the **jump cut** (usually a mistake, not a choice).

**Scaffolded questions. Cold-call through all three:**

> **Recall:** "What do the In and Out points on a clip actually control? And separately, what's
> the difference between an L cut and a J cut?"

> **Analysis:** "Why would an editor choose Overwrite instead of Insert when they know exactly
> where a new clip should go? Why does a cutaway help an editor hide an awkward edit point?"

> **Synthesis/Extension:** "Find a jump cut in something you've watched recently. Was it a
> mistake, or a deliberate style choice? How can you tell?"

## Guided Practice | Assemble, Then Refine (~26 min)

**Assemble:**

1. **Open your Unit 2 project** from 2.2 and confirm your bins are still organized.
2. **Set In and Out points** on at least 4 clips from your bins.
3. **Insert** your first 2 clips onto the timeline in order.
4. **Overwrite** your next clip directly into the middle of the sequence at a specific point.
5. **Practice a Lift and an Extract** on a clip you no longer want, and compare what each leaves
 behind.

**Refine, on the same sequence:**

6. **Add an L or J cut** somewhere audio and video don't need to change at exactly the same
 frame. Let one lead or trail the other.
7. **Insert a cutaway** to cover a rough edit point or add context.
8. **Check for accidental jump cuts**: if two adjacent shots of the same subject look nearly
 identical, fix or intentionally justify it.
9. **Turn and Talk:** trade seats with a partner and shuttle through each other's sequence using
 only JKL. Can you tell where each edit type and each continuity technique was used?

{: .highlight }
An L/J cut only "works" if the audio and video are genuinely offset from each other on the
timeline. If they still start and stop at the same frame, you haven't actually made one yet.

{: .discussion }
**Cold call check:** you're editing a two-person interview and the same speaker's face is on
screen for both their question and their answer. What continuity technique would make that feel
less static?
