# 5.3 | Recording & Voice-Over

## Key Terms

{: .vocab }
{% include vocab/add-recording-voiceover.md %}

A great script recorded badly is still a bad episode. Today's entirely about the technical side
of capturing clean audio — the boring-sounding work that makes everything after it possible.

## Direct Instruction (~10 min)

{% include worksheet.html file="add5-recording-voiceover-reference" title="5.3 Guided Notes: Recording & Voice-Over" presentation="/assets/presentations/add5-3.html" %}

> "Clipping isn't a style — it's a hard digital error that can't be fixed after the fact. Keep
> your levels healthy while recording, because you can always turn something up later, but you can
> never un-clip it."

Walk through: recording **room tone** (silence in your space, useful for editing later),
monitoring **levels** and **gain**, avoiding **clipping**, and recording a clean **voice-over**
take.

**Scaffolded questions — cold-call through all three:**

> **Recall:** "What is room tone, and why would you bother recording silence?"

> **Analysis:** "Why can't clipping be fixed in editing after the fact?"

> **Synthesis/Extension:** "If your levels are too quiet instead of clipping, is that a smaller
> problem? Why or why not?"

## Guided Practice — Record Clean Audio (~23 min)

1. **Record 10 seconds of room tone** — everyone silent, mic running.
2. **Record a scratch track** — a rough first pass of your script, just to test levels and pacing.
3. **Check your levels** — adjust gain so you're never in the red (clipping) but still clearly
   audible.
4. **Record your final voice-over take**, with a partner watching levels and calling out any
   clipping in real time.

{: .discussion }
**Cold call check:** halfway through your final take, you notice you've been clipping the whole
time. What do you do — keep going, or stop and restart?
