# 6.3 | Keying & Green Screen

## Key Terms

{: .vocab }
{% include vocab/add-keying.md %}

A green screen shot is only half the work—the real skill is making the key invisible, so the
composite looks like it was shot in one place, not two.

## Direct Instruction (~10 min)

{% include worksheet.html file="add6-keying-reference" title="6.3 Guided Notes: Keying & Green Screen" presentation="/assets/presentations/add6-3.html" %}

> "Ultra Key does the heavy lifting, but spill suppression is what separates an amateur composite
> from a clean one—that green glow around someone's hair or shoulders is the first thing a
> viewer's eye catches."

Walk through: **chroma key** vs. **luma key**, applying **Ultra Key**, sampling the key color, and
suppressing **spill** around edges and semi-transparent areas like hair.

**Scaffolded questions—cold-call through all three:**

> **Recall:** "What's the difference between a chroma key and a luma key?"

> **Analysis:** "Why does hair cause more keying problems than a solid subject like a jacket?"

> **Synthesis/Extension:** "If your footage was shot on a blue screen instead of green, what would
> you need to change about your approach?"

## Guided Practice—Key & Composite (~23 min)

1. **Import the provided green screen footage** and apply Ultra Key.
2. **Sample the key color** to remove the green background.
3. **Suppress spill** around the subject's edges, especially hair.
4. **Add a new background** and scale/position the subject so the composite looks intentional.
5. **Partner troubleshooting:** swap with a partner and identify one specific problem
   (spill/fringing/holes) in their composite.

{: .discussion }
**Cold call check:** your subject's hair has a visible green glow even after keying. What Ultra
Key control should you adjust first?
