# 1.5 | Film Form & Shot Vocabulary

## Introduction
Before you shoot your explainer video, you need a shared vocabulary for talking about shots. Directors, camera operators, and editors all use the same shot names so that a script or shot list means the same thing to everyone on set — even a set of one person and a phone.

## Shot Types
Shot type is decided by how much of the subject fills the frame.

Extreme Long Shot (ELS)
: Shows the subject as a small element within a vast environment, emphasizing location over subject.

Wide Shot (WS)
: Shows the full scene or setting, with the subject visible but sharing the frame with their surroundings.

Long Shot (LS)
: Shows the subject's entire body, with some surrounding space still visible.

Medium Shot (MS)
: Frames the subject from roughly the waist up. The most common shot for an explainer video host talking to camera.

Medium Close-Up (MCU)
: Frames the subject from the chest up — closer than a medium shot, still comfortable for extended talking.

Close-Up (CU)
: Fills the frame with the subject's face or a single object, drawing attention to detail and emotion.

Extreme Close-Up (ECU)
: Isolates a small detail — an eye, a hand, a button being pressed — so closely that context disappears.

Over-the-Shoulder (OTS)
: Frames a subject from behind another person's shoulder, common in interviews and conversations.

Point of View (POV)
: Shows what a subject is seeing, as if the camera were their eyes.

Cutaway
: A shot of something other than the main action, used to cover an edit or add context (for example, cutting to a close-up of a diagram while your voice-over keeps talking).

## Composition
### Rule of Thirds
Divide the frame into a 3x3 grid. Placing your subject along these lines or at their intersections is generally more visually interesting than placing them dead-center.

### Foreground, Midground & Background
Layering elements at different distances from the camera adds depth to a shot. A cluttered background can distract from your subject — check what's behind you before you hit record.

### Depth of Field
The zone of a shot that appears in sharp focus. A shallow depth of field (background blurry, subject sharp) draws the eye to your subject; a deep depth of field keeps everything in focus, which is useful when a diagram or object behind you also needs to be readable.

### Field of View
How much of a scene a lens captures, from wide (fits more in frame, exaggerates distance) to narrow/telephoto (fits less in frame, compresses distance). Most phone camera apps default to a wide field of view — step back rather than relying on digital zoom, which lowers image quality.

## Apply It
Before your shoot day, mark up your two-column script with the shot type for each shot (MS, CU, OTS, etc.). This gives you — and anyone helping you film — a fast reference on set, no extra equipment required.
