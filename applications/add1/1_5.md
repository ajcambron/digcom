# 1.5 | Film Form & Shot Vocabulary

## Introduction
Before you shoot your explainer video, you need a shared vocabulary for talking about shots. Directors, camera operators, and editors all use the same shot names so that a script or shot list means the same thing to everyone on set — even a set of one person and a phone.

## Shot Types
Shot type is decided by how much of the subject fills the frame, from an extreme long shot that shows a subject small within their environment, down through wide, long, medium, and close-up framings, to an extreme close-up that isolates one small detail. A medium shot (roughly waist-up) is the most common framing for an explainer video host talking to camera.

Beyond straight-on framings, an over-the-shoulder shot frames a subject from behind another person's shoulder (common in interviews), a point-of-view shot shows what a subject is seeing, and a cutaway briefly interrupts the main action to show something else related to the scene — useful for cutting to a diagram while your voice-over keeps talking.

## Composition
A rule of thirds grid — dividing the frame into thirds both ways — gives you lines and intersections that are generally more visually interesting places to put your subject than dead-center.

Layering your foreground (closest to the camera) and background (farthest from the camera) adds depth to a shot. A cluttered background can distract from your subject — check what's behind you before you hit record.

Depth of field describes how much of that foreground-to-background range stays in focus. A shallow depth of field (background blurry, subject sharp) draws the eye to your subject; a deep depth of field keeps everything in focus, which helps when a diagram or object behind you also needs to be readable.

Field of view is how much of a scene your lens actually captures. Most phone camera apps default to a wide field of view — step back rather than relying on digital zoom, which narrows your field of view by cropping and lowers image quality.

## Apply It
Before your shoot day, mark up your two-column script with the shot type for each shot (MS, CU, OTS, etc.). This gives you — and anyone helping you film — a fast reference on set, no extra equipment required.
