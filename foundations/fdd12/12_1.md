# 12.1 | Scale & Measurement in Design

## Key Terms

{: .vocab }
{% include vocab/fdd-scale-measurement.md %}

A design that looks right on screen can still be the wrong size in real life. Today's lesson is
about the math every designer needs to make sure that never happens.

## Bell Ringer (~10 min)

Without measuring, estimate the actual size (in inches) of 3 objects your teacher shows you — a
phone, a poster, a cereal box. We'll reveal the real measurements and see how close your
estimates were.

## Direct Instruction (~10 min)

{% include worksheet.html file="guided-notes-12-1" title="12.1 Guided Notes: Scale & Measurement in Design" presentation="placeholder" %}

Guided notes: **scale** is a design's size relative to its real, actual-size object, written as a
**ratio** (e.g., 1:2 means the drawing is half real size). A **scale ruler** has multiple sets of
markings, one per common ratio, so you can measure or draw at scale without doing the math for
every single measurement. Watch a worked example: converting an 8" × 12" package to a 1:2 scale
drawing.

{: .discussion }
Where does scale matter most in a designer's actual work — packaging, signage, print sizing,
somewhere else?

## Guided Practice (~23 min)

With a partner, work through the 2 practice conversion problems on your guided notes handout.
Check your answers against another pair before moving on.

## Studio/Project Work Time (~30 min)

1. Pick a real object (something you have with you, or one your teacher provides measurements
   for).
2. Choose a scale ratio (1:2, 1:4, or 1:10) and calculate the object's scaled dimensions.
3. In Illustrator, create a new document and set its exact width and height to your calculated
   scaled dimensions (not a preset — type in your own numbers).

{: .reflection }
Where would a designer's mistake with scale actually cause a real, expensive problem?

## Exit Ticket / Wrap-Up (~5 min)

Save your file, then take a screenshot showing your document's exact dimensions.
