# 1.4 | Communicating Design Plans

{: .vocab }
{% include vocab/fdd-design-plans.md %}

## Designers don't just make things — they show their plan first
Before a designer creates a final piece, they communicate the *plan* for it — through sketches,
wireframes, style guides, and project briefs — so the client can react and give feedback before
time is spent on a finished piece. This back-and-forth is the **feedback loop**, and it's how
every project in this class will work: sketch it, show it, revise it.

{: .discussion }
Why might it be a bad idea to build a finished design before showing anyone your plan for it?

## On Paper Today

1. **Warm-up (5 min):** on your handout, put these design-process steps in order: Draft, Sketch,
   Final, Feedback, Revise. (Trick: there's more than one correct order, depending on how many
   feedback loops happen — be ready to explain yours.)
2. **Guided practice:** look at the sample style guide page your teacher hands out. As a class,
   identify what it's communicating (colors, fonts, spacing rules) and why a designer would show
   this *before* building anything final.
3. **Independent practice:** sketch a one-page "plan" for a made-up project (e.g., a poster for a
   school club) that includes: a thumbnail sketch, one labeled color choice, and one sentence
   describing who the audience is. This is a plan, not a finished design — it should look rough.

{: .reflection }
If you handed your one-page plan to a client, what's one question you'd expect them to ask you
back?

📤 **Exit Ticket:** turn in your one-page plan sketch with all three required parts (thumbnail,
color choice, audience sentence).
