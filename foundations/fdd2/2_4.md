## Direct Instruction (~10 min)

{% include worksheet.html file="guided-notes-2-4-pixels-vs-paths" title="2.4 Guided Notes: Pixels vs. Paths" presentation="/assets/presentations/fdd2-4.html" %}

> "Every photo you've ever taken is a raster image, a fixed grid of colored squares. Zoom in far
> enough and you'll always eventually see the blocks. A vector graphic doesn't have that limit,
> because it isn't stored as a grid at all. It's stored as a set of mathematical instructions
> Illustrator recalculates every time you zoom or resize. Your logo has to live at 48 pixels wide
> AND 225 pixels wide, in the same file. Raster can't do that without looking blocky at one end
> or blurry at the other. Vector can, that's the whole reason Illustrator is the standard tool for
> logos."

**Scaffolded questions. Cold-call through all three:**

> **Recall:** "What's the difference between a raster image and a vector graphic, in your own
> words?"

> **Analysis:** "Your Design Brief requires the same mark to work at 48px and at 225px wide. Why
> does that specific requirement rule out building it as a raster image from the start?"

> **Synthesis/Extension:** "If a classmate insists their raster sketch 'looks fine, I'll just
> resize it,' what would you actually show them to prove the problem?"

## Bauhaus Shape Exercise | Generate Ideas (~8 min)

{% include worksheet.html file="fdd2-4-bauhaus-shape-exercise" title="2.4 Bauhaus Shape Exercise" %}

**In groups of 3-4:** act out each of Bauhaus teacher Johannes Itten's three form characters
before drawing anything, a square (trace it corner to corner in the air, tense), a triangle
(sharp, all angles), a circle (one continuous curve). Itten taught this exact sequence: experience
each form bodily first, then imagine it, then draw it.

> "Heart and hand must be one. For a genuine idea to show up in a sketch, it has to be felt first,
> not just copied from a reference."

Then apply Kandinsky's angle-color correspondence as a starting rule (not a hard requirement):
acute angles (45°) read as yellow, right angles (90°) read as red, and obtuse angles/curves (135°,
heading toward a circle) read as blue. Sketch 3 thumbnails combining 2–3 basic shapes into one
simple mark, small, about favicon size. If a thumbnail doesn't read clearly that small, simplify
it before moving on.

{: .discussion }
Which form character, square, triangle, or circle, fits the personality you want your portfolio
site to have? Why?

## Guided Practice | Design Development (~23 min)

{% include worksheet.html file="guided-notes-2-4-design-aids" title="2.4 Guided Notes: Rulers, Guides, Grids & Views" %}

1. **Circle your strongest thumbnail** from the Bauhaus Shape Exercise. That's what you build
 today, not a new idea.
2. **Build it digitally** on your Favicon artboard using Illustrator's shape tools from Unit 1
 (Rectangle, Ellipse, Polygon, Star, Line Segment). No Pen tool yet, that's Unit 4.
3. **Use at least one design aid**: rulers, a guide, the grid, or Smart Guides, to align and size
 your shapes with real precision, not by eye.
4. **Test the favicon-readability rule**: shrink your artwork view to a small size on screen.
 Does the mark still read clearly? If not, simplify further.
5. **Extend into the Header Logo artboard**: combine your mark with your site's name as simple
 type, roughly following the 5.625:1 proportions from 2.3's research.
6. **Partner critique:** trade screens and check each other's mark against the favicon test
 before finishing.

{: .discussion }
Which design aid (rulers, guides, grid, or Smart Guides) felt most useful for building precise
geometric shapes specifically, and why might that be different from what felt useful in Unit 1's
freer S1 project?
